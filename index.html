Module 3: Giving feedback that helps

How to give feedback people can use

Most feedback fails for the same reason: the other person cannot tell what to keep doing or what to change. This page shows a simple structure you can use in any conversation, then lets you test it.

Page 2 of 6

What you will be able to do

By the end of this page you will be able to:

  1. Describe the four parts of useful feedback.
  2. Tell vague, harsh and useful feedback apart.
  3. Choose a specific, observable way to open a feedback conversation.

Four parts of useful feedback

Use the parts in this order. Each one answers a question the other person is already asking.

Context: name the moment

Tie the feedback to one specific time and place so the other person can picture it too. Without context, people defend their character instead of discussing an event.

Example: "In Tuesday's client call, when we reached the budget question..."

Observation: describe what you saw or heard

Stick to what a camera could have recorded. "You interrupted the client twice" is an observation. "You were rude" is a judgement, and judgements start arguments.

Example: "...you answered before the client finished the question, twice."

Impact: explain the effect

Say what happened as a result, for you, the team or the work. Impact turns a comment into a reason to change.

Example: "After that, the client stopped sharing details, and we still do not know their real budget."

Next step: agree what happens next

Ask for their view first, then agree one concrete action. Feedback that ends with a shared next step is far more likely to change anything.

Example: "How did the call feel from your side? What would help in Thursday's follow-up?"

Same situation, three approaches

A teammate sent the monthly report a day late, and two charts used last year's figures. Here is the same message delivered three ways.

Vague

"Good effort on the report, but it could be a bit tighter. Let's step it up next time."

Why it misses: nothing here says what went wrong or what to do differently. Your teammate leaves reassured and unchanged.

Harsh

"This report was late again and the numbers were wrong. I can't keep checking your work."

Why it backfires: it names the problem but aims at the person. "Again" and "your work" invite a defence, not a fix.

Useful

"The monthly report reached me on Thursday, a day after we agreed, and two charts used last year's figures. I had to ask the director to move our review. Can we look at your workload tomorrow and agree a date that works for next month?"

Why it works: context, observation, impact and a next step, with no judgement of the person.

Myth or fact?

Decide what you think about each statement before you read the answer.

  • Statement

    Starting with praise makes criticism easier to hear.

    Answer

    Myth

    People learn to wait for the "but", so the praise stops counting. Give praise and correction separately, each with its own specifics.

  • Statement

    Feedback works best soon after the event.

    Answer

    Fact

    Details are still fresh for both of you, so the conversation stays on specifics instead of impressions.

  • Statement

    If someone has not asked for feedback, they do not want it.

    Answer

    Myth

    Most people want to know how they are doing. Ask first, for example "Can I share an observation about the meeting?", so they can choose the moment.

Real-world scenarios

Four situations where the same four parts help. For each one, notice which part does the most work.

  1. The quiet teammate

    A new teammate has said almost nothing in three planning meetings. Before deciding they are not interested, ask privately what would make it easier to contribute, and describe what you noticed rather than what you assume.

  2. The repeated mistake

    The same error has appeared in two shift handovers. Name both occasions, show the effect on the next shift, and agree one check together. Two specific examples land better than "you always".

  3. Feedback for your manager

    Your manager often changes priorities late on Friday. Ask for ten minutes, describe one recent example and its effect on the team's Monday deadlines, then propose a cut-off time that works for both of you.

  4. Praise that teaches

    A colleague calmed an angry caller. "Good job" fades fast. "You repeated the caller's concern back before offering options, and they relaxed" tells them exactly what to do again.

Check your understanding

Two quick questions. Nothing is scored or saved.

Question 1: choose one answer

A teammate's slides had several errors in yesterday's client presentation. Which opening is most useful?

Answers appear when JavaScript is enabled.

Show the answer key

Answer: "In yesterday's presentation, three slides showed last quarter's figures, and the client asked about them twice."

It names the moment and describes what happened without judging the person. The other openings are a judgement, too vague to act on, or about everyone else.

Question 2: select all that apply

Which of these make feedback more likely to help? Select all that apply.

Answers appear when JavaScript is enabled.

Show the answer key

Answer: describing what you saw or heard, explaining the effect it had, and agreeing a next step together.

Those three give the person facts, a reason and a way forward. Comparing people turns feedback into a ranking, and by the annual review the details are gone.

Accessibility notes

This page is a sample built to WCAG 2.1 AA. Every interactive part is a native HTML control first, and JavaScript adds behaviour on top. With JavaScript off or stripped by an editor, all content still shows and no button is left that does nothing.

What was built, and to which pattern

  • Accordion: W3C ARIA Authoring Practices (APG) Accordion. A button inside each heading, with aria-expanded and aria-controls. Panels have no region role, because several can be open at once and a landmark for each would clutter navigation.
  • Tabs: APG Tabs with automatic activation. Only the selected tab is in the Tab order; arrow keys, Home and End move between tabs.
  • Flip cards: no ARIA pattern exists, so each card has one plain button whose name says what it does, for example "Show answer: feedback works best soon after the event". Only one side is shown at a time, so text reflows when enlarged.
  • Carousel: APG Carousel. Rotation is off until you start it, stops as soon as keyboard focus or the pointer is inside it, and slides are announced only when you change them yourself.
  • Checkpoint questions: native radio buttons and checkboxes in a fieldset. Feedback appears in a status message (WCAG 4.1.3) with an icon and words, and focus stays where you are.
